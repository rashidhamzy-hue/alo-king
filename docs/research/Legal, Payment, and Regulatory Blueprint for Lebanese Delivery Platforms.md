# **Legal, Financial, and Regulatory Architecture for a Food-Delivery Platform in Lebanon**

Operating an on-demand food-delivery platform in Lebanon requires navigating a commercial landscape shaped by dual-currency retail mechanics, non-bank financial clearing rails, evolving electronic commerce statutes, and strict statutory corporate governance. Functioning as a two-sided digital marketplace, the platform coordinates transactions among consumers, restaurant operators, and motorcycle couriers, intersecting directly with commercial contract law, consumer and food hygiene standards, fiscal invoicing mandates, and labor classification rules.

## **Alternative Payment Rails: Whish Money and OMT Merchant and Disbursement Integration**

Given that domestic commercial banking channels remain constrained for routine consumer clearing, mobile wallets and licensed money transfer operators have emerged as the primary digital transaction infrastructure in Lebanon1. For an on-demand delivery enterprise, financial architecture requires two distinct payment flows: consumer-facing digital collections (pay-in) and automated vendor and courier disbursements (pay-out)1.  
Whish Money operates under the regulatory supervision of Banque du Liban (BDL) as an electronic money institution, providing both digital collection and bulk remittance solutions1. For consumer checkouts, the enterprise integrates the Whish Pay application programming interface (API), which facilitates in-app payments through deep linking directly into the customer's Whish mobile wallet or through hosted checkout pages supporting card-funded wallet transactions2.  
For outgoing cash flows—specifically the settlement of weekly net receivables to partner restaurants and daily earnings to couriers—the platform interfaces with the Whish Corporate Solutions Disbursement API3. This service allows the automated execution of bulk transfers using the beneficiary's registered mobile telephone number in either US Dollars (USD) or Lebanese Pounds (LBP), eliminating the security hazards and operational friction associated with physical cash distribution3.  
Online Money Transfer (OMT), historically established as the principal Western Union agent in Lebanon, provides enterprise merchant services through OMT Pay and its proprietary corporate payment gateway1. OMT accommodates both direct digital wallet checkouts and code-based offline settlements2. Under the code-based model, the delivery platform issues an automated numerical billing reference via API; the diner visits any of OMT’s physical locations across Lebanon to tender cash, triggering a real-time server-to-server webhook that confirms payment to the platform2.  
For outbound disbursements, OMT’s Corporate Cash-Out API enables platforms to issue authenticated payment orders allowing couriers and restaurant representatives to collect cash over the counter across OMT's branch network1.  
Securing production API credentials from both Whish Money and OMT requires completing institutional Know-Your-Customer (KYC) underwriting1. Non-bank financial operators are bound by BDL Circulars on anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorist financing (CFT), requiring applicants to operate as formally registered commercial entities7.  
The onboarding process requires submitting a verified corporate portfolio, including:

* An official extract of the Commercial Register (*Sejel Tijari*) issued by the competent Court of First Instance within the previous three months7.  
* The official Commercial Circular (*Iza’a Tijariyya*) certifying active management authority and authorized company signatories7.  
* The registered Articles of Association (*Statuts*) defining the company's legal objectives7.  
* The Ministry of Finance Tax Registration Certificate establishing the fiscal identification number (*Raqam Mali*)7.  
* Government-issued identification for all authorized signatories and ultimate beneficial owners (UBOs) holding ten percent or more of equity7.  
* Proof of operational premises, verified through a registered commercial lease or property deed supported by a recent utility invoice (e.g., OGERO or Électricité du Liban)7.  
* A bank account confirmation certificate issued by a licensed Lebanese commercial bank verifying an active corporate account for fund settlements7.

Following document verification, both institutions require technical security reviews1. Platform engineering teams must demonstrate encrypted payload handling (TLS 1.3), webhook signature verification via hashed message authentication codes (HMAC), and dedicated sandbox testing before being granted production API keys1. Commercial pricing generally incorporates an onboarding setup charge alongside volume-based transaction processing fees ranging from 1.0% to 2.5%, subject to contractual negotiation3.

While cash-on-delivery and local mobile wallets process the majority of daily orders, supporting credit and debit card acquiring remains essential for capturing expatriate transactions, corporate catering, and higher-income customer segments1. Operating card acceptance in Lebanon requires selecting between domestic card acquirers and regional or international payment gateways1.

Areeba remains the central domestic card acquirer and processor in Lebanon, operating under BDL oversight1. Areeba issues direct merchant agreements that settle transactions into the business’s local Fresh USD account1.  
Because Lebanese commercial banks enforce rigorous underwriting standards, securing an acquiring line through Areeba involves detailed operational scrutiny, including reviews of the platform’s dispute mitigation procedures, projected gross merchandise value (GMV), refund automation, and PCI-DSS compliance1. The platform must confirm that raw primary account numbers (PAN) are tokenized through Areeba's hosted fields or payment sheets rather than handled directly on application servers1.  
Enterprises that lack access to domestic commercial banking sometimes attempt to use international payment gateways by establishing holding entities in jurisdictions like the United States, the United Kingdom, or the United Arab Emirates, utilizing merchant aggregators such as iShip12.  
While this model enables immediate compatibility with international processors like Stripe, it creates structural operational friction12. Domestic Lebanese cardholders face cross-border transaction surcharges, and the local delivery platform faces corporate transfer-pricing liabilities under Lebanese income tax law when repatriating operating capital to settle local restaurant accounts12. For domestic operations, partnering directly with a local acquirer like Areeba or NetCommerce provides the most sustainable clearing structure1.

## **Dual-Currency Checkout Mechanics: Fresh USD, LBP Legal Tender, and Rate Volatility**

The macroeconomic environment in Lebanon requires food-delivery applications to implement a dual-currency pricing and settlement engine1. Managing this operational flow requires balancing retail pricing practices with mandatory currency regulations.  
Article 192 of the Lebanese Code of Money and Credit establishes the Lebanese Pound as legal tender within the republic, penalizing anyone who refuses payment in the national currency. However, in response to severe currency volatility, the Ministry of Economy and Trade (MoET) issued regulatory decrees permitting retail and food-service establishments to denominate menu prices in US Dollars to stabilize consumer pricing.  
This pricing policy is conditioned on two statutory requirements:

> 1. The exchange rate applied to convert USD prices to Lebanese Pounds must be displayed clearly to consumers throughout browsing and checkout flows1.  
> 2. Consumers retain the unrestricted legal right to settle their transaction in Lebanese Pounds at the stated operational rate, whether paying through digital payment rails or upon physical delivery1.

To maintain compliant operations, the technical platform should maintain its central menu and catalog database in US Dollars, insulating core menu engineering from currency shifts1.  
The consumer interface must dynamically present the subtotal, logistics fee, and applicable taxes in USD alongside an exact, real-time conversion into LBP calculated using the platform’s published operational exchange rate1. Under MoET transparency guidelines, this operational rate must align with prevailing market exchange rates to avoid administrative sanctions for deceptive retail pricing.  
A persistent operational risk for delivery platforms is cash-on-delivery (COD) split-currency settlement1. In practice, Lebanese consumers frequently settle bills using a combination of currencies, tendering partial cash in physical USD banknotes and the remainder in Lebanese Pounds1.  
To prevent accounting discrepancies, the courier application must feature a split-tender collection interface1. When marking an order as fulfilled, the courier enters the exact amounts collected in each currency1. The backend accounting ledger immediately updates the courier’s shift balance, tracking currency amounts separately to eliminate exchange discrepancies1.  
Furthermore, to insulate the business from intraday currency shifts between order placement and doorstep handover, the platform’s terms of service must explicitly define the transaction's exchange rate as fixed at the exact timestamp of digital order confirmation.

## **E-Commerce Governance and Personal Data Protection under Law No. 81/2018**

Lebanese Law No. 81 of October 10, 2018, relating to Electronic Transactions and Personal Data, forms the legislative framework governing online commercial contracts, consumer disclosures, and digital record processing11.  
Electronic commercial contracting is governed under Title I and Title II of the law, which grant electronic agreements and digital confirmations legal validity and evidentiary weight equal to traditional paper instruments11. To ensure that digital orders remain legally binding, the platform’s checkout process must follow an affirmative order confirmation sequence11:

* **Mandatory Pre-Contractual Disclosures**: Before checkout completion, the application must disclose full commercial identity details, including the registered company name, Commercial Registry number, physical corporate address, direct customer support contacts, comprehensive pricing breakdowns, delivery surcharges, and order cancellation terms2.  
* **Order Review and Verification Mechanism**: The consumer interface must present an unalterable summary screen outlining the ordered menu items, drop-off address coordinates, selected payment method, and total charges, providing a clear interface for correcting input errors before final confirmation11.  
* **Affirmative Double-Click Consent**: Passive or inferred assent, such as pre-selected checkboxes, does not establish binding consent under the law11. The user must perform an active confirmation step, such as clicking an unambiguous button labeled *"Confirm Order and Authorize Payment"*11.  
* **Automated Transaction Confirmation**: Immediately following transaction processing, the platform’s systems must dispatch an unalterable digital receipt via SMS, email, or a WhatsApp utility message template confirming order specifications2.

Title V of Law 81/2018 regulates the collection and processing of personal data11. Unlike regulatory frameworks that rely on an independent data protection authority, enforcement in Lebanon falls under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Economy and Trade18.  
Under Article 87, personal data collection must be limited to explicit, lawful purposes directly tied to operational fulfillment11. Gathering customer records—such as precise GPS tracking coordinates, names, delivery addresses, and purchasing histories—is legally permitted only to fulfill deliveries, maintain operational accounts, or comply with statutory accounting requirements11. Using this data for targeted promotional broadcasts or sharing it with third-party advertising vendors requires renewed affirmative opt-in consent11.  
Data subjects are granted statutory rights under Articles 92, 99, and 10111. Consumers maintain the legal right to access their stored personal records, receive verified disclosures regarding data processing workflows, correct inaccurate information without charge, and demand data deletion when their account is closed or when records are no longer required for service delivery11. The platform's operational infrastructure must be designed to fulfill these statutory data requests within ten business days11.  
Under Article 93, data controllers must implement technical safeguards to prevent unauthorized access or system breaches, requiring platforms to maintain TLS 1.3 encryption in transit and AES-256 encryption at rest for customer profiles and transactional records1.  
Article 94 requires data controllers managing systematic personal data collections to submit a formal declaration to the Ministry of Economy and Trade11. While routine, transactional databases maintained strictly for basic corporate accounting and payroll benefit from statutory exemptions under Article 94(5), operating a consumer marketplace that profiles geographic data, processes high-frequency mobile orders, and routes records to international cloud infrastructure (such as AWS, GCP, or Azure) falls within the scope of Article 9411. Consequently, the platform must submit a formal declaration describing its data architecture, cloud hosting locations, and security safeguards to the MoET11.

## **Consumer Protection and Public Health Standards in Food Logistics**

Food delivery marketplaces operate at the intersection of consumer rights and public health safety standards. Physical fulfillment failures—such as food-borne illnesses, delivery delays that spoil temperature-sensitive meals, or unsealed packaging—expose the business to liability under Lebanese consumer protection and public health laws.  
Consumer Protection Law No. 659 of February 4, 2005, establishes statutory standards for commercial honesty, price transparency, and dispute resolution. Digital intermediaries are held accountable for misleading marketplace listings2.  
The platform’s menu displays must disclose ingredient profiles and common food allergens (such as nuts, dairy, and gluten) as reported by kitchen partners. The platform must also avoid hidden surcharges; all fees, including service commissions, delivery fees, and municipal charges, must be itemized before payment authorization2.  
Furthermore, Law 659/2005 requires platforms to provide operational customer redress channels, such as automated refund systems or order replacement pathways, to resolve complaints regarding damaged, spoiled, or missing orders2.  
Logistical transport standards are governed by Food Safety Law No. 35 of 2015, enforced by the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH) and the Ministry of Agriculture. The law regulates hygiene standards throughout the storage and transit phases of the food supply chain:

* **Thermal Management During Transit**: Hot prepared foods must be maintained at or above ![][image1] throughout transit, while cold or perishable dishes must remain below ![][image2]. Couriers must use insulated, partitioned thermal delivery boxes to prevent temperature fluctuations and cross-contamination between hot meals and chilled beverages.  
* **Tamper-Evident Packaging Requirements**: The platform's merchant agreements must require partner restaurants to package all orders in containers secured with tamper-evident adhesive seals. This practice provides a clear operational chain of custody: broken seals place responsibility on the courier and platform, while intact seals preserve the kitchen's liability for preparation defects.  
* **Courier Health and Hygiene**: Couriers must comply with municipal health and hygiene guidelines, ensuring that delivery equipment, insulated bags, and vehicle compartments are sanitized daily.

## **Fiscal Invoicing, Value-Added Tax (VAT), and Revenue Reconciliation**

Structuring a digital delivery marketplace requires maintaining a strict fiscal separation between the platform's core operating revenues and the gross value of food merchandise passing through its systems15. Conflating gross merchandise turnover with company earnings can expose the business to severe corporate tax liabilities and VAT penalties15.  
Value-Added Tax (VAT) in Lebanon is governed by Law No. 379/2001, as amended by subsequent national budget legislation26. The standard national VAT rate is 11%26.  
A major fiscal change occurred under the **2024 National Budget Law (Law No. 324\)**, which increased the mandatory VAT registration threshold from LBP 100,000,000 to **LBP 5,000,000,000** (Five Billion Lebanese Pounds) measured over one to four consecutive fiscal quarters26. Platforms whose cumulative taxable earnings exceed this threshold are required to register with the VAT Directorate at the Ministry of Finance and file quarterly tax returns9.  
From a fiscal perspective, an on-demand food platform acts as a technology intermediary providing matching, ordering, and logistics services. It does not purchase, take title to, or resell food inventory.  
Consequently, its accounting ledger must treat transaction flows through three distinct categories:

> 1. **The Food Component**: The partner kitchen is the legal seller of the meal and remains responsible for issuing a fiscal receipt for the food total and remitting any applicable food-service VAT to the tax authority.  
> 2. **The Logistics Delivery Fee**: The delivery fee paid by the diner represents gross logistics turnover for the platform. If the platform exceeds the statutory VAT threshold of LBP 5,000,000,000, it must charge and remit 11% VAT on this logistics fee26.  
> 3. **The Software Marketplace Commission**: The commission fee deducted from restaurant sales (e.g., 15% to 20% of food GMV) constitutes taxable B2B revenue earned by the platform. The platform must issue periodic tax invoices to the restaurant detailing this service fee plus 11% output VAT28.

| Transaction Component | Contractual Provider | Paying Party | Fiscal Documentation Issued | Fiscal and VAT Classification |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Gross Food Cost** | Restaurant Partner | Consumer / Diner | Restaurant Fiscal Receipt / Guest Check | Restaurant's gross retail revenue; accounted for under the kitchen's VAT profile. |
| **Delivery Surcharge** | Delivery Platform Fleet | Consumer / Diner | Electronic Order Receipt11 | Platform logistics turnover; subject to 11% VAT if platform exceeds LBP 5B threshold26. |
| **Platform Take-Rate (Commission)** | Delivery Platform | Restaurant Partner | Periodic B2B Commercial VAT Invoice | Platform technology service revenue; 11% VAT charged to restaurant28. |
| **Cash-on-Delivery Collected** | Logistics Courier | Platform / Restaurant | Shift Reconciliation Ledger1 | Balance-sheet transit of third-party funds; exempt from corporate income tax1. |

Cash-on-delivery payments collected by couriers represent third-party escrowed funds held in transit1. The platform must maintain distinct balance-sheet ledger accounts that separate logistics revenues and commissions from restaurant sales receipts.  
Without unambiguous contractual clauses designating the platform as an intermediary collection agent, the Ministry of Finance’s tax audit division could classify total gross food volumes as platform operating turnover, exposing the business to 17% corporate profit tax assessments on aggregate transactions rather than net commission revenues15.

## **Fleet Logistics: Employment versus Freelance Courier Classification**

Structuring the delivery fleet involves addressing critical legal liabilities under Lebanese labor and administrative law. Selecting between direct salaried employment and an independent contractor (freelancer) model carries significant financial, social security, and operational ramifications under Lebanese jurisprudence.  
The Lebanese Labor Code of 1946 does not contain specific statutory provisions for platform or "gig economy" workers. As a result, workers are classified under one of two traditional legal frameworks: formal employees (*Ujara’*) governed by the protective provisions of the Labor Code, or independent contractors operating under bilateral service agreements governed by the Lebanese Code of Obligations and Contracts (*Qanun al-Mujabat wal-Uqud*).  
When reviewing contractual relationships, Lebanese labor courts apply the **Legal Subordination Doctrine** (*Rabitat al-Taba’iyya al-Qanuniyya*). Courts evaluate actual working arrangements rather than the formal titles used in written agreements.  
Judicial reclassification of independent couriers into formal employees is generally triggered by several operational factors:

* Requiring fixed work schedules, mandatory login hours, or minimum weekly shift commitments.  
* Restricting couriers from delivering for competing aggregators or platforms during active hours.  
* Mandating the use of corporate uniform apparel, company delivery boxes, and branded vehicle graphics.  
* Implementing algorithmic penalties, disciplinary pay deductions, or automated account suspensions for rejecting dispatches.

A judicial finding of employment misclassification exposes the platform to substantial liabilities with the National Social Security Fund (*Al-Sunduq Al-Watani Lil-Daman Al-Ijtima'i* \- NSSF)9.  
Under statutory contribution schedules, employers must remit monthly contributions totaling 21.5% of gross wages (comprising 8% for sickness and maternity, 6% for family allowances, and 8.5% for the End-of-Service Indemnity account), while deducting an additional 3% from employee compensation for health coverage.  
A successful reclassification claim triggers retroactive contribution assessments across the worker's tenure, combined with statutory late-payment penalties of up to 100% of the underlying balance. Terminated couriers may also claim statutory End-of-Service Indemnities equal to one month’s final wage per year of service, alongside compensation for arbitrary dismissal under Article 50 of the Labor Code.  
A related operational challenge involves vehicular registration rules. Under the Lebanese Traffic Law (Law No. 243/2012), commercial transportation operations require vehicles registered with public commercial registration plates (*Lawhat Hamra* / Red Plates)32.  
A substantial majority of delivery drivers in Lebanon operate private motorbikes bearing standard private registration plates (white plates). Law enforcement agencies regularly conduct targeted enforcement sweeps, impounding private motorcycles used for commercial delivery and issuing fines32.  
To minimize disruption, the delivery platform should either require contracted couriers to show proof of a commercially registered vehicle (*Darraja Nariyya Tijariyya* with red plates) or partner with licensed third-party logistics (3PL) transport companies that maintain compliant commercial fleets32.  
Furthermore, under Legislative Decree No. 136/1983 governing occupational accidents, businesses can be held liable for workplace injuries sustained during service delivery. Given the road safety risks associated with motorcycle transport in Lebanon, independent contractor agreements do not insulate a business from civil tort claims if a courier suffers severe injury or causes third-party harm during an order delivery. The platform must maintain a commercial third-party motor liability and courier group-accident insurance policy through an authorized domestic insurer.

## **Operating Permits and Administrative Licensing**

Establishing a fully compliant delivery platform requires securing multiple administrative licenses and municipal permits across several regulatory authorities:

* **Commercial Registration (*Sejel Tijari*)**: Mandatory registration executed before the Clerk of the Commercial Court of First Instance in the judicial district where the corporate seat is located (e.g., Beirut or Mount Lebanon), officially establishing the legal entity9.  
* **Ministry of Finance Matriculation (*Raqam Mali*)**: Acquisition of the unique fiscal identification number for corporate income tax declarations, followed by VAT registration once gross taxable revenue surpasses the LBP 5,000,000,000 threshold9.  
* **Municipal Operating License (*Rokhsa Baladiyya*)**: Issued by the competent local municipality (e.g., the Municipality of Beirut) authorizing the physical operation of corporate offices, dispatch command hubs, and customer service facilities. Operating without a valid municipal permit risks administrative closure and fines by the municipal police (*Harass al-Baladiyya*).  
* **Ministry of Public Health Food Logistics Notification**: Formal registration of the delivery operation as a food-handling logistics establishment under Food Safety Law No. 35/2015, certifying transit hygiene and thermal equipment compliance.  
* **Ministry of Telecommunications Authorizations**: If the platform deploys independent short-code SMS systems or dedicated telecommunications routing rather than third-party Cloud APIs, appropriate routing authorizations must be secured through the Ministry of Telecommunications and OGERO.

## **Minimum Company Structure and Mandatory Legal Retainer Governance**

Selecting the appropriate corporate structure determines whether an enterprise can open commercial bank accounts, secure merchant payment gateway access, protect founders from business liabilities, and maintain regulatory compliance under the Lebanese Code of Commerce9.

| Corporate Legal Structure | Statutory Minimum Capital | Partner / Shareholder Boundaries | Personal Liability Profile | Institutional Underwriting & Banking Viability | Governance Requirements & Administrative Overhead |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Sole Proprietorship (*Mu’assasa Fardiyya / Tajer Fard*)** \[cite: 9, 30\] | No statutory minimum capital9. | Exactly one individual owner9. | **Unlimited**: Personal assets (savings, personal property) remain entirely exposed to corporate debts and tort liabilities9. | Unfavorable: Domestic card acquirers (Areeba) and financial payment providers (OMT) generally decline sole proprietorships for enterprise API integration1. | Lowest formation costs; basic commercial books required; no board meetings or formal statutory corporate filings9. |
| **Limited Liability Company (*S.A.R.L. \- Société à Responsabilité Limitée*)** \[cite: 15, 16, 30, 33\] | **LBP 5,000,000** (\~\$56 at prevailing market valuation)15. | 1 to 20 partners (Single-member SARL explicitly permitted under Law No. 126/2019)9. | **Limited**: Partner liability is strictly capped at individual capital contributions15. | **High**: Standard corporate vehicle accepted by all commercial banks, payment acquirers, and wallet providers1. | Standard corporate profit tax rate of 17%; mandatory appointment of a retained Bar attorney; statutory auditor required only if specific capital or turnover thresholds are exceeded15. |
| **Joint Stock Company (*S.A.L. \- Société Anonyme Libanaise*)** \[cite: 15, 16, 30, 33\] | **LBP 30,000,000**15. | Minimum of three shareholders9. | **Limited**: Shareholder exposure is limited to the nominal value of equity held15. | **Highest**: Standard vehicle for institutional venture financing and large-scale enterprise operations9. | High formation costs; mandatory Board of Directors with a Lebanese national majority; mandatory court-appointed statutory auditor; mandatory retained Bar attorney15. |

The most practical corporate vehicle for an early-stage food-delivery platform in Lebanon is the **Limited Liability Company (S.A.R.L.)**9:

* **Shielding Founders from Liability**: Logistics and food-service operations carry daily liabilities, including traffic collisions, food safety issues, and payment disputes. Registering as a sole trader (*Tajer Fard*) is ill-advised, as commercial liabilities attach directly to the founder’s personal assets9. An SARL establishes a distinct legal identity that protects the founders' personal assets from corporate debts15.  
* **Single-Partner Corporate Formation**: Following amendments introduced by **Law No. 126 of March 29, 2019**, to the Lebanese Code of Commerce, an SARL can be established by a single partner (*Sharika bi-Chakhs Wahid*), eliminating the historical mandate requiring three founding members9.  
* **Institutional Banking and Gateway Eligibility**: Operating on-demand payment gateways with domestic acquirers (Areeba, NetCommerce) and electronic money networks (Whish Money, OMT) requires a registered corporate entity with a Commercial Register certificate, an active tax number, and a corporate bank account1.

A central governance requirement under Lebanese commercial law is the **Mandatory Legal Retainer Mandate**9. Under the Law Regulating the Legal Profession (Law No. 8/1970, as amended) and the Code of Commerce, all limited liability companies (S.A.R.L.) and joint stock companies (S.A.L.) incorporated in Lebanon must retain a licensed attorney registered with either the Beirut Bar Association or Tripoli Bar Association as a permanent legal counsel under an annual retainer contract (*Muhamin Mustashar*)9.  
This legal retainer requirement impacts operations in several ways:

* The Commercial Registry will not finalize corporate registration or release the official Commercial Registration Certificate without an executed retainer contract stamped by the relevant Bar Association30.  
* The Bar Association establishes a mandatory minimum annual retainer fee that the company must pay its designated attorney36.  
* The company must maintain an active retainer throughout its operational lifecycle9. If counsel resigns or is replaced, the company must register a successor attorney within thirty days to avoid administrative fines and registry suspensions9.

## **Critical Operations Requiring Professional Legal Counsel**

To ensure regulatory compliance and insulate the business from significant financial and civil liabilities, management must engage qualified legal counsel for several specific operational and corporate matters:

* **Corporate Incorporation and Bar-Stamped Retainer (Statutory Requirement)**: Lebanese law requires an attorney to oversee company incorporation, draft the corporate Articles of Association (*Statuts*) with appropriate operational clauses, secure official stamps from the Bar Association, and complete registration with the Commercial Registry and Ministry of Finance9.  
* **Independent Courier Contractor Agreements and Labor Risk Mitigation**: Counsel is needed to draft courier service contracts that avoid the legal markers of operational subordination under the Lebanese Labor Code. Properly structured agreements help insulate the platform from reclassification claims by labor courts and unexpected retroactive contribution demands from the National Social Security Fund (NSSF)9.  
* **Electronic Terms of Service and Checkout Enforceability (Law No. 81/2018)**: Legal review is necessary to ensure the platform’s digital consumer terms, double-click checkout flows, and operational disclosures meet the requirements of Law 81/2018 and Consumer Protection Law 659/2005, preserving the legal enforceability of digital contracts11.  
* **Personal Data Protection Filings (Ministry of Economy and Trade)**: Counsel should prepare and submit the formal data processing declaration required under Article 94 of Law 81/2018 to the MoET, documenting geolocation tracking, customer data storage, and cross-border cloud hosting arrangements11.  
* **Restaurant Master Services Agreements and Agency Structuring**: An attorney must draft the core B2B merchant contracts to explicitly establish an intermediary agency relationship for order matching and payment collection. This contractual separation prevents the Ministry of Finance from treating gross food sales as platform operating turnover, protecting the business from inappropriate VAT and corporate income tax assessments15.
